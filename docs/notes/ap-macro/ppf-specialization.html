Chapter 3 · PPF, Growth & Comparative Advantage

The production possibilities frontier (PPF) captures scarcity, efficiency, and opportunity cost in a single diagram—then sets up the logic for specialization and trade.

Reading the PPF

  • Points on the curve: Efficient use of resources; economy cannot produce more of one good without sacrificing the other.
  • Inside the curve: Unemployment or underused resources.
  • Outside the curve: Currently unattainable with existing resources and technology.

Why the PPF is Bowed Out

The law of increasing opportunity cost explains curvature.

  • Resources are specialized; some inputs are better for good A than good B.
  • Moving resources away from their best use yields smaller and smaller gains.
  • Diminishing returns make each additional unit of one good cost more of the other.

Shifts in the Frontier

  • Resource changes: Population growth, new capital, or natural discoveries move the PPF outward.
  • Technology/productivity: Better techniques push the frontier outward; sector-specific advances pivot the curve.
  • Negative shocks: Natural disasters, war, or policy mistakes can shift the PPF inward.

Specialization & Trade

Countries gain when they specialize according to comparative, not absolute, advantage.

Absolute Advantage

The ability to produce more with the same inputs. Example: Country A can grow more bananas per worker.

Comparative Advantage

Lower opportunity cost. If Country B gives up only 0.5 apples per banana while Country A sacrifices 2 apples, B has the comparative advantage in bananas.

Gains from trade: Specialization allows both nations to consume outside their own PPFs.

Efficiency Checkpoints

  • Productive efficiency: Output produced at the lowest possible cost.
  • Allocative efficiency: The specific mix of goods most valued by society.
  • Competitive markets: Push producers toward both efficiencies by rewarding lowest-cost combinations of resources.
