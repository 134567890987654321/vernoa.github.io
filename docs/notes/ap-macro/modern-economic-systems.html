Chapter 1 · Economic Systems & the Core Problem

This chapter frames the AP Macro year: why modern economies look nothing like traditional societies, and how scarcity forces every system to make choices.

From Traditional to Modern Europe

  • Traditional era: Low GDP, tiny productive capacity, stagnant living standards, and vulnerability to population shocks or war.
  • Property rights revolution: Private ownership, enforceable contracts, and the rule of law let capital accumulate and productivity climb.
  • Modern economies: Voluntary exchange dominates, but policy and policing still steer markets when failures appear.
  • Key theme: Living standards track productivity; how we produce and distribute income determines progress.

How Systems Answer the Three Questions

Every system must decide what to produce, how to produce, and for whom.

  • Traditional: Customs, roles, and inheritance keep output predictable but resist innovation.
  • Command: Government planners dictate prices, output, and distribution; useful in crisis but often inefficient.
  • Market (Laissez-faire ideal): Prices emerge from private choices; minimal regulation, high responsiveness.
  • Mixed: Market allocation with targeted commands when markets break down (safety, defense, externalities).

Debate continues: What level of regulation best balances efficiency with equity?

Productivity & Income Distribution

  • Small productive capacity → low real GDP per capita: Limited capital and weak technology chain people to subsistence levels.
  • Growth comes from: Better technology, human capital gains, and efficient labor/capital markets.
  • Distribution matters: Rapid GDP growth can still leave inequities if income is concentrated.

Scarcity: Unlimited Wants vs Limited Resources

Scarcity drives economics. Society cannot satisfy every want with finite land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurial talent.

  • Economics: The social science that studies how people allocate scarce resources to maximize welfare.
  • Choice implies cost: Choosing one goal means giving up the next-best alternative.
  • Macro vs Micro:
    • Macro: Nation-level outcomes—growth, unemployment, inflation.
    • Micro: Individual markets—firms, households, price signals.

Positive vs Normative Economics

Positive statements: Descriptive, testable claims about "what is." Example: "Higher payroll taxes reduce labor supply."

Normative statements: Value-laden prescriptions about "what should be." Example: "Students should receive more homework each day."

AP questions will often ask you to identify whether a statement is positive or normative.
